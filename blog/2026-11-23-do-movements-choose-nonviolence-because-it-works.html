Do movements choose nonviolence because it works? Also: intent as AI’s missing abstraction

Research / Theory

Strategic versus Principled Nonviolence

Oftentimes, scholars of nonviolence and contentious politics ask, “How effective is nonviolence?” They make arguments that nonviolence is more or less effective than violence for certain reasons. There is value to these questions. But they don’t always specify what type of nonviolence is being used.

By type, I mean the mental model or beliefs, explicit or implicit, of the practitioners about the use of violence vis-à-vis their goals. I also mean the models of the audience for nonviolence. These two things, and the distinction between them, could significantly change the findings that these questions produce.

I believe this should be separated into two camps with different implications for finding an ‘effect’: principled nonviolence and strategic nonviolence (which can be combined). Combining these in analysis causes major issues for answering the theoretical question, as strategic nonviolence directly implies purposive selection into nonviolence.

Principled nonviolence

Principled nonviolence views the use of nonviolence in contentious politics as deontological. This means the correctness of nonviolence is independent of its effectiveness, either in an aggregate sense or in a specific sense. Typically, there would be a spiritual, ideological, or religious preference for and belief in nonviolence as being good, regardless of its effect.

Even deontological or principled nonviolence may have gradations. The principle of nonviolence may come into conflict with other deontological goals, such as some kind of end state for society or some sort of effect on a certain group. In these cases, nonviolence could still be forsaken for violence if it conflicts with other principles seen as more pressing.

In these cases, however, the distinction between principled and non-principled nonviolence is that, while some other value superseded nonviolence, nonviolence would still have been a better path to that objective. All else equal, if nonviolence could have solved the dilemma, that would have been better. So the principle still stands.

Strategic nonviolence

If principled nonviolence says that nonviolence is correct regardless of its effect, strategic nonviolence says that nonviolence is good because it works, because it’s effective.

Paragons of nonviolence have been explicitly strategic – not principled – about nonviolence, directly stating they would switch if nonviolence were less effective. Aung San Suu Kyi had this revealing dialogue on the topic:

SUE LAWLEY (interviewer): Nevertheless, Aung San Suu Kyi, Nelson Mandela himself changed, didn’t he? He said that non-violence as a tactic should be abandoned when it no longer worked. Is that something you might be tempted by? AUNG SAN SUU KYI: It’s possible because I have said in the lectures that I do not hold to non-violence for moral reasons, but for practical and political reasons, because I think it’s best for the country. And even Ghandi, who is supposed to be the father of non-violence, said that between cowardice and violence, he’d choose violence any time BBC Reith Lecture 2011

Duties and competing principles

There are a few different philosophical branches that nonviolence can fit into, but I think the two easiest mappings are deontological and utilitarian. Oftentimes, from a political science perspective, these things can look the same or very similar.

The classic deontological example is Kant, who says there’s a single categorical imperative and everything is downstream of it. This is a single deontological stance that must be applied to all situations. A pluralist view of deontological commitment would be that there are multiple different values or duties that must be upheld. When these conflict, either it’s a true dilemma and there’s no correct answer, or they have some kind of ranking according to the scenario. This is not necessarily a utilitarian ranking. It could be a categorical ranking.

In the case of nonviolence, people who hold a principled or deontological view could give up nonviolence in their actions, taking the violent route when they view other duties as threatened. These might include the right or duty of self-defense, or the duty of ensuring some political goal like equity or democracy.

Just because a person or group stops being nonviolent does not mean they’ve given up on nonviolence. They may still hold nonviolence as a deontological commitment but view other things as more important. A principled practitioner could use or not use nonviolence in a specific scenario without implying that they’ve given up on nonviolence.

The audience and the strategic preference

The audience is operative in strategic concerns, as most nonviolent benefits flow through being perceived as being morally advantaged (Chenoweth and Stephan 2013). Someone could also be using nonviolence because others view nonviolence as better. Putting the reason aside, others may prefer nonviolence. This could be a principled, utilitarian, or strategic preference of the audience or any outside actor, including the state.

If nonviolence is seen as the best path to an objective, but neither good nor bad in and of itself, that’s a strategic view of nonviolence. One of its most famous practitioners is Aung San Suu Kyi, quoted above. She was looking at the role of nonviolence in her context. If she were placed into a different context – including Myanmar in post-2021 – she may have embraced violence. Critically, this selection is endogenous to context.

When nonviolence has no route to working

We can do a thought experiment and create scenarios where nonviolence simply would not work. Say you had an unintelligent machine operating violence, with no ability to communicate with it. It would clearly be worth carrying out violence against that machine from a strategic perspective.

Now say the machine had a person inside who was blind and deaf, who knew what the machine was doing, but with whom you could in no way communicate. You have a person intentionally operating the machine to put down a rebellion who cannot be communicated with. From a strategic perspective, nonviolence will never be as effective as violence in this scenario because nonviolence has no route to working. The person operating the machine doesn’t even know nonviolence is taking effect. Violence would be more effective in that case.

Another scenario is when that person can be communicated with but is sociopathic and does not care. Again, in this toy scenario, we can say that violence is clearly more effective.

There are situations in real life where, because of pressures on the military, pressures on the state, or some other reason, nonviolence is not likely to be effective from a political standpoint. Studies that look at whether nonviolence is effective on average do not invalidate specific situations where nonviolence would be less effective.

Many arguments for why nonviolence is effective are contingent on things like an audience, or others recognizing the voice of nonviolent actors as legitimate in the public space. If they simply don’t acknowledge the discourse or moral valence of claimants practicing nonviolence, no amount of nonviolence will ever work.

What changes a practitioner’s willingness to use violence?

Practicing nonviolence for principled versus utilitarian reasons has clear implications. If a person in a machine was deaf and blind and could not possibly be reasoned with, a principled nonviolent practitioner would refrain from doing violence against this person because the commitment to nonviolence was never premised on its effectiveness. Its ineffectiveness here would not, all else equal, change the permissibility of using violence for the same aims.

To make it more concrete, say I’m a practitioner of nonviolence and I believe in democracy. Independently of how effective violence would be compared to nonviolence in achieving democracy, I believe it is always correct to use only nonviolence. If nonviolence does not work to get democracy, I would still remain nonviolent.

When someone can be reasoned with, the likelihood of democracy may go up. When someone cannot be reasoned with, is not otherwise amenable to nonviolence, or doesn’t have a moral sensibility that nonviolence is meant to trigger, my willingness to step into violence should not go up.

Now say a different duty arose, such as the duty to prevent a catastrophe, where I had to stop someone from releasing a nuclear bomb into a population. Maybe I would be willing to use violence because this is a different categorical duty. But that is independent of the effectiveness of violence.

A strategic practitioner says that nonviolence has certain benefits, but these are contingent on the political opportunity around its use. As nonviolence becomes less effective as a strategy, their adherence to it goes down and their willingness to contemplate other things goes up.

Why this matters for measuring effectiveness

Actors are selecting nonviolence from a menu of strategies in relation to their aims. Either a principled or a strategic practitioner may use violence. In the principled case, they may still hold nonviolence as a value but have a higher-order value that conflicts with it. In the strategic case, they give up on nonviolence because it becomes less effective in attaining their goal or the end state they actually value.

It may not be that nonviolence is more effective, but that practitioners select nonviolence when it is more effective for their goals, whether principled or strategic. The key difference is that, in the strategic case, it is not the goal or duty that has shifted, but the likelihood of a certain outcome. In the principled case, some other competing principle has outweighed it.

This may confound measurements that do not have true variation and randomness. Hypothetically, what would we need to test whether violence or nonviolence is more effective? We would need plausibly exogenous, not strategic, selection of violence vs. nonviolence across settings. That is, some external force which ‘tips’ movements from one to the other. It is hard to imagine what this might be, as movements are explicitly strategic endeavors. Additionally, variations in selection may also be correlated with variation in outcomes. For instance, during the Arab Spring, not only the movement but also governments may shift their perceived efficacy of nonviolence. If the same event shifts both the choice of tactic and the outcome, it cannot serve as an instrumental variable (the exclusion restriction is violated).

Tooling

Intent as a missing abstraction

  • With LLMs the ‘session’ is a basic abstraction for how work gets done. However, the session itself has only two parts: 1) stateless model + 2) stateful context (tokens). Context may be tools, system instructions, etc. but these are all tokens that change the next token (i.e. fundamental system instructions are special tokens that change what the output is).
    • Note: harnesses are not part of sessions, though they interact with them by wrap sessions with deterministic code (code which, of course, can compose calling other agents in other sessions). This is extremely powerful.
  • Intent is something outside of sessions: the effect that the human is attempting to actualize through working with sessions.
  • Intent is almost never well captured by harnesses. Not only is it poorly captured as a first-class primitive, but the expression of intent is extremely lossy both in what solution the human wants to solve exactly and how the human wants to solve it exactly.
  • One of the hardest problems with working with LLMs is that an incredibly well-specified piece of intent is almost like casting a spell. It creates an aesthetically pleasing cathedral from nothing. However, equally so, weak expression of intent – where the human does not communicate or maybe even know what they really want – produce ‘oatmeal’. Here, oatmeal is the bland center of the training distribution which all the models are trained on.
  • A seemingly fruitful design problem is how LLM’s themselves can work with a human to boot-strap high quality intent – this is the attempt at ’/grill-me and other skills which seek to extract intent.
  • However, this extraction seems unlikely to succeed unless it can fill in the areas where the human’s domain expertise is actually weak. You can not ask the human to give you specific intent that they cannot draw on the domain-specific details to imagine (i.e. i cannot ask an LLM to create an excellent movie, because I do not even have the vocabulary for what makes a movie great. This is not taste as judgment, it’s a domain vocabulary which allows expressing a vision of what does not exist precisely.) This almost always requires not only domain expertise but extracting some unique aspect of ‘vision’ from the human without contaminating the human with oatmeal-ness.

You’re an executive, but whither the AI-firms?

  • This relates to an idea Sam Schillace has been incubating in his newsletter that we are ‘all executives now’. Being an executive is unlike other work because you own only the intent and outcome, but all the process and work is otherwise mediated through organizational interfaces. This is what hierarchy was created to solve: giving executives meso-level interfaces for processing information to interact with. This is what ‘orchestrators’ are in the AI world, they are middle management, and you are the executive.
    • However, building tools for AI-organizational management is still a motley aggregation of prototypes. There are no standard patterns for what the primitives of an organization should be ( but lots of opinionated prototypes! e.g.: pstack, swamp, mainplane).
  • Executives are effective when they can achieve their intent through indirect management. The indirectness is what lets organizations scale, while effective management means intent is not lost (or inefficient) due to coordination costs. Fundamentally, executives work through hierarchy.
  • Alex Imas et al. make the case that the ‘binding constraint’ for LLM’s ability to transform our economy is that they are poor at information coordination on large tasks -– i.e. they are bad at working in hierarchies. This caps the Coasean ceiling for their size, since they get weak returns from decomposing tasks to multiple agents. Currently LLMs are not post-trained to coordinate. Thus, they have very weak information processing compared to human organizations, in which middle management – despite its reputation – is the critical information processing interface between executives and agents.
